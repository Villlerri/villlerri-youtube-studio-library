COLD OPEN

On the fourth of November, 1825, a fleet of boats gathered off Sandy Hook, at the entrance to New York Harbor.

On one of them, Governor DeWitt Clinton emptied a keg into the Atlantic. The water in that keg came from Lake Erie, more than five hundred miles away by water, on the far side of a mountain range.

They called it the Wedding of the Waters.

Eight years earlier, New York State had started digging a ditch across its own wilderness to make that moment possible. Critics mocked it as the big ditch. It went down in history as Clinton's Ditch.

In the decades after that keg was emptied, New York City pulled away from every rival port in the country.

So how does a ditch make a city? To see it, we need the whole map.

KEY

A quick key before we start. A solid line is documented. A dashed line is a reconstruction. Hatching marks what is disputed. And time runs along the bottom.

CHAPTER 1. THE WALL

In 1800, the United States was a country of the coast. Its biggest cities, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore and Boston, were all seaports. And they were rivals.

Behind them rose the Appalachians: ridge after ridge, from New England down to Alabama.

Beyond the mountains, the country was filling up. Settlers poured into Kentucky, Tennessee and Ohio. They grew wheat and corn, and raised hogs.

But their harvest had a problem. The cheapest way to market was not east, over the mountains. It was downstream: by flatboat down the Ohio and the Mississippi, all the way to New Orleans. For a farmer in Ohio, the cities of the Atlantic coast might as well have been overseas.

Over land, everything was slow and expensive. Around 1817, moving one ton of goods from Buffalo to New York City, by wagon and then by river, cost close to a hundred dollars. And it took weeks.

Washington did build one road west, the National Road. It reached the Ohio River at Wheeling in 1818. It helped. But a wagon on a good road was still a wagon.

Water was the answer. In 1816, Clinton's canal petition put it in numbers. Carrying a ton a hundred miles over land cost about thirty-two dollars. By canal, he promised, it would cost one. The question was where water could get through the mountains.

Look closer at New York. The Hudson River runs north from the harbor for about a hundred and fifty miles, so low that the ocean tide reaches all the way to Troy.

At Albany, the Mohawk River comes in from the west, through a valley between the Adirondacks and the Catskills. Follow the Mohawk, then the lowlands beyond it, and you reach the Great Lakes without crossing a mountain ridge. The way is not flat. But it is the only low gap through the Appalachian wall. Nature had drawn most of a line from the Atlantic to the interior. Someone just had to finish it.

CHAPTER 2. CLINTON'S DITCH

The idea was not new. People had dreamed of a canal through this valley for decades. In the 1790s, a private company built a few small locks around the falls of the Mohawk. It helped, but not enough.

What was new was the scale: one artificial river from the Hudson at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo. Three hundred and sixty-three miles, through forest, swamp and rock.

Why go all the way to Lake Erie, when Lake Ontario is closer? Because a boat on Lake Ontario could just as easily sail down the St. Lawrence to Montreal, in British Canada. The planners wanted the trade of the West to have one way out: through New York. So the line runs inland, all the way to Erie.

Its champion was DeWitt Clinton: former mayor of New York City, canal commissioner, and, from 1817, governor.

Washington would not pay for it. In March 1817, President Madison vetoed a bill that would have funded roads and canals. Thomas Jefferson is said to have called the plan "little short of madness". We have that line only from a New York legislator, recalling their meeting years later.

So New York paid for it alone. In April 1817, the legislature voted to begin. On the Fourth of July, 1817, digging started at Rome, in the flat middle of the state, where fast progress could convince the doubters.

The canal itself was modest. Forty feet wide at the surface. Four feet deep. With a towpath alongside for the horses and mules that pulled the boats.

Its engineers had never built a canal. Benjamin Wright and James Geddes were surveyors who learned on the job. A young assistant, Canvass White, went to England to study its canals. Back home, he found a local stone that made cement that hardens under water. That cement held the locks together.

The digging was done by hand and by animal, section by section, by local contractors. Most of the laborers were men from nearby farms and towns. Irish immigrants worked alongside them. In 1819, fever in the marshes near Montezuma laid up more than a thousand men.

The name Clinton's Ditch stuck. In the end, it stuck as a compliment.

CHAPTER 3. THE CLIMB

Here is the problem the builders faced, drawn by their own hands.

In 1821, the canal commissioners published this map. With it comes a profile: the height of the land from the Hudson to Lake Erie.

Lake Erie sits about five hundred and sixty-five feet above the Hudson. Water does not run uphill. So the canal had to climb in steps, and each step is a lock.

A lock is a box of water with gates at both ends. A boat floats in, and the gate closes behind it. Water flows in from the level above, and the boat rises with it. Then the upper gate opens, and the boat floats out, one step higher. Going down, it all runs in reverse.

The gates meet in a shallow V, pointing upstream. The weight of the water presses them shut.

The canal needed eighty-three locks. Many of them climb the Mohawk Valley. In the middle of the state, one level runs for almost seventy miles without a single lock.

But at the western end, the land rises in one long wall: the Niagara Escarpment, the same ridge that the Niagara River falls over.

At a place soon called Lockport, the builders cut two staircases of five locks each into the rock, side by side, lifting boats about sixty feet. One staircase for boats going up, one for boats coming down. Above them, crews blasted a channel through solid rock: the Deep Cut.

Rivers were the other obstacle. At Rochester, the canal crossed the Genesee River on a stone aqueduct eight hundred feet long: a bridge that carried water instead of a road.

Section by section, the line came together. Boats were paying tolls on the finished middle section in 1820, five years before the whole canal was done.

CHAPTER 4. TEN DAYS IN 1825

On October twenty-sixth, 1825, the canal opened from end to end.

At ten in the morning, at Buffalo, Governor Clinton boarded a canal boat called the Seneca Chief. As it set off, a cannon fired. Then another, within earshot down the line. Then another. Cannons placed along the canal and down the Hudson passed the signal from gun to gun. It reached New York City in about an hour and twenty minutes.

The boats themselves moved at the speed of a walking horse, and down the Hudson behind a steamboat. Nine days of crowds, speeches and fireworks. On November fourth they reached New York, and a fleet escorted them out to Sandy Hook.

New York published a whole book about the celebration, with pictures like this one. New York merchants gave Clinton a pair of silver vases, in thanks for the canal. Potters in England put the canal on dinner plates and sold them to Americans.

It was a victory party. The real victory came later.

CHAPTER 5. THE FLOOD

First, a twist. Counted within its city limits, New York was already the largest city in America before the canal was dug. In 1810, the census found about ninety-six thousand people in New York, and about fifty-four thousand in Philadelphia. Count Philadelphia's whole county, though, and it was still bigger than New York until the 1820s. The race was not over.

The canal did not make New York first. It made New York impossible to catch.

Once the canal opened, the cost of moving a ton of goods between Buffalo and New York fell by about nine tenths. A trip that took weeks now took days.

Traffic poured in. Wheat and flour came east, first from western New York, and soon from Ohio and beyond. Manufactured goods and settlers went west. In 1820, New York was a distant third among American ports in flour exports. By 1827, it led.

Tolls flowed into the state treasury. Within about a decade, they had paid back what the canal cost to build: about seven million dollars.

Along the line, villages became cities. Rochester, with its mills on the falls of the Genesee, became the Flour City. Buffalo grew from a frontier village into a great lake port.

And at the end of the line, New York City grew fastest of all. In 1820, about one hundred and twenty-four thousand people lived there. By 1860, more than eight hundred thousand did.

The canal was not the only reason. New York already had a deep, sheltered harbor. From 1818, the Black Ball Line sailed to Liverpool on a fixed schedule. State law favored New York's auction houses, where British goods were sold off cheaply. And cotton from the South passed through New York's merchants and banks.

Historians still argue how much of New York's rise belongs to the canal. But the canal gave New York something no rival had: a water road into the heart of the continent. It made every other advantage stronger, because now the produce of the West came to New York's docks.

Philadelphia and Baltimore fought back with canals and railroads of their own. But they had to climb the mountains. New York had gone around them.

CHAPTER 6. THE SAME GAP

Clinton's ditch was soon too small. The state began to widen and deepen it in 1836. Railroads followed the same route: up the Hudson, along the Mohawk, across the lowlands to Buffalo. In 1853, ten small lines between Albany and Buffalo merged into the New York Central. Later, the highway took the same path.

Clinton's ditch itself was widened, moved and rebuilt more than once. The canal that runs there today is its successor.

But the gap in the mountains never moved. And the city at its mouth is still the largest in America.

One ditch, four feet deep, did not build New York by itself. But it decided where the water would go. And for a trading city, that was everything.
